HVAC SERVICE TECHNICIAN REFERENCE MANUAL
Residential Split Systems


SECTION 1: THE REFRIGERATION CYCLE

An air conditioner does not create cold. It moves heat from one place to
another. Understanding this single idea prevents most of the diagnostic
mistakes new technicians make.

The cycle has four main components arranged in a closed loop. Refrigerant
travels continuously through all four, changing state as it goes.

The compressor is the pump of the system. It takes low pressure refrigerant
vapour from the suction line and raises both its pressure and its temperature.
It sits in the outdoor unit and draws more electrical current than any other
component. A compressor is designed to pump vapour only. Liquid refrigerant
entering the compressor cannot be compressed and will destroy the valves or
break the connecting rods. This failure mode is called slugging.

The condenser is the coil in the outdoor unit. Hot high pressure vapour enters
at the top and gives up heat to the outdoor air as a fan pulls air across the
fins. As the refrigerant loses heat it condenses into a liquid. By the time it
leaves the bottom of the condenser it should be entirely liquid and slightly
cooler than its condensing temperature. That temperature difference is called
subcooling.

The metering device creates the pressure drop that makes the whole cycle work.
High pressure liquid enters, and a sudden restriction causes a sharp drop in
pressure on the other side. Common types are the fixed orifice or piston, the
capillary tube, and the thermostatic expansion valve. A thermostatic expansion
valve, usually shortened to TXV, adjusts the flow based on the temperature of
the suction line, which makes it far more adaptable than a fixed orifice.

The evaporator is the indoor coil. Low pressure liquid refrigerant enters and
immediately begins to boil, absorbing heat from the indoor air blown across it
by the blower. The refrigerant leaves as a vapour, slightly warmer than its
boiling point. That temperature difference is called superheat. The vapour
returns through the suction line to the compressor and the cycle repeats.


SECTION 2: REFRIGERANTS

R-22, a hydrochlorofluorocarbon, was the residential standard for decades.
Production and import in the United States ended on 1 January 2020 under the
Montreal Protocol. Existing systems can still be serviced using recovered or
stockpiled stock, but prices have risen sharply. Its ozone depletion potential
is 0.055 and its global warming potential is 1810.

R-410A is a hydrofluorocarbon blend that replaced R-22 in most residential
split systems. It has zero ozone depletion potential but a global warming
potential of 2088. It operates at roughly fifty to seventy percent higher
pressure than R-22, which means equipment, gauges, hoses and recovery machines
must all be rated for it. R-410A is a near azeotropic blend and must always be
charged into the system as a liquid, never as a vapour, or the blend will
fractionate and the composition will shift.

R-32 is a single component refrigerant with a global warming potential of 675.
It is classified A2L, meaning mildly flammable. It requires leak detection and
specific installation practices but is increasingly common in new equipment
because of its much lower environmental impact.

R-454B is an A2L blend with a global warming potential of around 466. It is
being adopted widely as the replacement for R-410A in new residential
equipment manufactured from 2025 onward.

Refrigerants must never be mixed. A system that has been charged with the
wrong refrigerant, or topped up with a different one, must have its entire
charge recovered, then be evacuated to a deep vacuum, then recharged with the
correct refrigerant by weight.

Venting refrigerant to the atmosphere is illegal under Section 608 of the
Clean Air Act. Technicians must hold EPA Section 608 certification to purchase
or handle refrigerant.


SECTION 3: SUPERHEAT AND SUBCOOLING

Superheat is the amount of heat added to refrigerant vapour above the
temperature at which it boiled. To measure it, take the suction line pressure,
convert that pressure to a saturation temperature using a pressure temperature
chart or app, then measure the actual line temperature and subtract the
saturation temperature from it.

Target superheat on a fixed orifice system at the evaporator outlet is
typically eight to twelve degrees Fahrenheit, though the correct target
changes with indoor wet bulb and outdoor dry bulb temperature. Manufacturer
charging charts should be used where available.

Low superheat means the evaporator is being overfed with refrigerant. The
refrigerant is not fully boiling before it leaves the coil, so liquid is
travelling back toward the compressor. This condition is called flooding and
it is one of the fastest ways to destroy a compressor.

High superheat means the evaporator is being underfed. Common causes are an
undercharge, a restriction in the liquid line, a failed metering device, or a
plugged filter drier.

Subcooling is the amount of heat removed from liquid refrigerant below its
condensing temperature. Measure the liquid line pressure, convert to
saturation temperature, then subtract the actual liquid line temperature from
that saturation temperature.

Target subcooling on a TXV system is typically ten to fifteen degrees
Fahrenheit, measured at the liquid line leaving the condenser.

Low subcooling usually indicates an undercharge or a liquid line restriction.
High subcooling usually indicates an overcharge or a restricted metering
device holding refrigerant back in the condenser.

The rule is simple. On a system with a TXV, charge by subcooling. On a system
with a fixed orifice or capillary tube, charge by superheat.


SECTION 4: AIRFLOW

The standard design airflow for residential cooling is four hundred cubic feet
per minute per ton of capacity. In humid climates this is often reduced to
three hundred and fifty cubic feet per minute per ton, which lets the coil run
slightly colder and remove more moisture from the air.

One ton of cooling capacity equals twelve thousand British thermal units per
hour.

Low airflow causes the evaporator coil to operate colder than designed. If the
coil surface drops below freezing, condensate turns to ice and the coil
freezes solid. Capacity collapses and liquid refrigerant may return to the
compressor once the coil is fully blocked.

The most common causes of low airflow, in the order a technician should check
them, are a dirty air filter, a dirty evaporator coil, closed or blocked
supply registers, crushed or undersized flexible ductwork, a dirty blower
wheel, and a failing blower motor or capacitor.

Total external static pressure should be measured across the air handler using
a manometer, with probes placed in the return before the filter and in the
supply after the coil. Most residential equipment is rated for one half inch
of water column total external static pressure. A reading above zero point
eight inches indicates a serious duct restriction that must be addressed
before any charging work is done.

Measuring static pressure before touching the gauges is a habit that separates
experienced technicians from parts changers. Airflow problems produce
refrigerant readings that look exactly like charge problems.


SECTION 5: EFFICIENCY RATINGS

SEER stands for Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio. It measures cooling output
over a typical cooling season divided by the electrical energy used in the
same period. Higher numbers mean better efficiency.

SEER2 replaced SEER in the United States on 1 January 2023. The test procedure
changed to use a higher external static pressure, which better reflects real
installed conditions. Because the test is harder, SEER2 numbers run roughly
four and a half percent lower than the old SEER number for identical
equipment. A unit formerly rated 15 SEER is approximately 14.3 SEER2.

The current minimum efficiency for residential split system air conditioners
is 13.4 SEER2 in the northern region and 14.3 SEER2 in the southern region.
The southern region carries a higher minimum because of longer cooling
seasons.

HSPF, and its replacement HSPF2, measure heating season performance for heat
pumps using the same seasonal approach.

EER measures efficiency at a single fixed operating condition, usually ninety
five degrees Fahrenheit outdoor temperature. It is useful for comparing
performance on design days rather than across a season.


SECTION 6: COMMON SERVICE FAULTS

A frozen evaporator coil is almost always caused by low airflow or low
refrigerant charge. Check the filter first, then measure airflow and static
pressure, and only then connect gauges. The system must be fully thawed before
any pressure readings will be meaningful.

Compressor short cycling, where the unit starts and stops rapidly, can be
caused by an oversized system, a low pressure switch tripping on undercharge,
a dirty condenser coil raising head pressure until the high pressure switch
opens, a failing run capacitor, or a thermostat located in a poor position.

Water leaking from the indoor unit is usually a blocked condensate drain line.
Clear the line with nitrogen or a wet vacuum, then check that the drain pan is
not cracked and that the float switch operates correctly. A frozen coil that
has thawed can also overflow the pan.

Warm air from the supply registers while the outdoor unit appears to run
suggests a failed run capacitor, a tripped high pressure switch, a burnt
contactor, or a complete loss of refrigerant charge.

An outdoor unit that hums but does not start is most often a failed run
capacitor. A capacitor that has swollen, leaked, or reads significantly below
its rated microfarad value should be replaced.

A tripping breaker points to a shorted compressor winding, a grounded
compressor, a seized fan motor, or a loose connection creating heat at the
lug. Never simply reset a breaker repeatedly without diagnosing the cause.

Ice forming on the liquid line rather than the suction line indicates a
restriction at that point, commonly a plugged filter drier or a kinked line.


SECTION 7: EVACUATION AND CHARGING

After any repair that opens the sealed system, the system must be evacuated
to remove air and moisture. Moisture combines with refrigerant and oil to form
acids that destroy the compressor from the inside.

Evacuate to five hundred microns or lower using a micron gauge, not the
compound gauge on a manifold set. Isolate the pump and confirm the reading
holds below one thousand microns for several minutes. A reading that rises
steadily indicates a leak. A reading that rises and then levels off indicates
remaining moisture.

Charge by weight whenever the manufacturer provides a charge figure and the
line set length is known. Add or subtract refrigerant for line set length
according to the data plate. Verify the final charge using subcooling on a TXV
system or superheat on a fixed orifice system.

Always charge liquid refrigerant into the liquid line with the system off, or
meter liquid slowly into the suction line through a metering device while the
system runs. Never allow raw liquid into the suction line of a running
compressor.
